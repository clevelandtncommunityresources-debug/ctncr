Our land · Cleveland, Bradley County & the region

We live on Cherokee land.

Red Clay, a few miles south of downtown Cleveland, was the last capital of the Cherokee Nation in the East. The forced removal called the Trail of Tears was run from here. Here is the story of this place, how to learn more, and the groups nearby doing the kind of work we do.

Cleveland, TN Community Resources works on the homelands of the Cherokee people, the Aniyvwiya (ᎠᏂᏴᏫᏯ), the Principal People, who were forced from this valley in 1838.

Long before them, other Native peoples farmed, fished and built towns along the Hiwassee and Tennessee rivers. We honor the Cherokee who were taken from here, and the Cherokee Nation, the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians and the United Keetoowah Band, who are still here today. Mutual aid isn’t new to this land: sharing food, labor and care with neighbors was a way of life here long before us.

1832–1838

Red Clay serves as the seat of the Cherokee Nation’s government. Eleven general councils meet there, each drawing 4,000 to 5,000 people.

16,000

About that many Cherokee were forced west in 1838–39. Thousands died in the camps and on the trail. Many estimates put the toll near 4,000.

3 nations

The Cherokee Nation and United Keetoowah Band in Oklahoma, and the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina.

The Cherokee in this valley

For centuries the Cherokee lived across what is now East Tennessee, western North Carolina, north Georgia and beyond. The Overhill towns along the Little Tennessee and Hiwassee rivers, about an hour north of Cleveland, included Chota, the Cherokee “mother town,” and Tanasi, which gave Tennessee its name. Nancy Ward (Nanyehi), the Beloved Woman of Chota, spoke for peace and for keeping Cherokee land; she is buried near Benton in Polk County.

Sequoyah, born near Vonore around 1770, created a writing system for the Cherokee language, the syllabary you see on this page. Within a few years thousands of Cherokee could read and write in their own language, and in 1828 the Nation began printing a newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix.

As Georgia moved to seize Cherokee land, the Nation moved its council to Red Clay, just over the state line in what is now Bradley County. Its sacred Blue Hole Spring (also called Council Spring) still flows there. Principal Chief John Ross led the Nation’s fight in the courts and in Congress, and won before the Supreme Court in 1832. The U.S. went ahead anyway.

The Trail of Tears started here

In 1838 the U.S. Army ran the Cherokee removal from Fort Cass, at the old Cherokee Agency on the Hiwassee River in Charleston. Soldiers took families from their homes at gunpoint and held thousands in camps that stretched south through the valley toward Cleveland, including at Rattlesnake Springs. Hundreds died in the camps from disease and bad conditions before the march west began that fall. While families were held a few miles away, Cherokee land was being sold off.

Some Cherokee escaped into the mountains or held onto land in North Carolina. Their descendants are the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians. In 1984, the Cherokee Nation and the Eastern Band met together in council at Red Clay for the first time since removal, and lit an eternal flame that still burns there.

Read more: “The Trail of Tears Started Here” from CBFP.news, the Trail of Tears National Historic Trail (National Park Service), and the history of Red Clay. To see whose homelands any place sits on, try Native Land Digital.

Learn from Cherokee people

The best source on Cherokee life, language and history is Cherokee people themselves. The Cherokee Nation Language Department and the Eastern Band’s Kituwah Preservation & Education Program teach the language. See the moon’s Cherokee names on our Weather & Sky tab.

History of Cleveland, Bradley County & the region

From mound-building towns to the Trail of Tears to today. Dates are from the sources linked at the bottom.

  1. 1000s–1500s

    Mississippian towns

    People of the Mississippian culture farm corn and build towns with earthen platform mounds along the Tennessee and Hiwassee rivers, such as at Hiwassee Island near the mouth of the Hiwassee.

  2. 1540

    The Spanish arrive

    Hernando de Soto’s army marches through the region, taking captives and food. The diseases Europeans bring kill huge numbers of Native people in the years after.

  3. 1700s

    The Overhill Cherokee

    Cherokee towns along the Little Tennessee and Hiwassee rivers, with Chota as a capital, trade and fight with the British and French. Yuchi, Shawnee and Muscogee (Creek) people also live in or travel through the region.

  4. 1776–1794

    The Chickamauga resistance

    Led by Dragging Canoe, Cherokee who refuse to give up more land move to towns near present-day Chattanooga and fight settler expansion for nearly 20 years.

  5. 1819

    The Hiwassee becomes a border

    The Calhoun Treaty takes the land north of the Hiwassee River. The federal Cherokee Agency sits on the river at what is now Charleston. Land to the south, including today’s Cleveland, is still Cherokee.

  6. 1821–1828

    A Cherokee renaissance

    Sequoyah’s syllabary spreads fast. The Nation adopts a written constitution in 1827 and starts the Cherokee Phoenix in 1828.

  7. 1830

    The Indian Removal Act

    Congress passes President Jackson’s plan to force Native nations west of the Mississippi.

  8. 1832

    Red Clay

    Barred from meeting in Georgia, the Cherokee move their council to Red Clay. The Supreme Court rules for the Cherokee in Worcester v. Georgia, but the ruling isn’t enforced.

  9. 1835

    The Treaty of New Echota

    A small group with no authority to speak for the Nation signs away all Cherokee land in the East. More than 15,000 Cherokee sign a petition against it. The Senate ratifies it anyway.

  10. 1836

    Bradley County is created

    Tennessee makes Bradley County out of the taken land, named for Col. Edward Bradley. Cleveland, named for Revolutionary War colonel Benjamin Cleveland, is laid out as the county seat.

  11. 1838

    Removal

    Soldiers round up the Cherokee into stockades and camps, with Fort Cass in Charleston as headquarters and camps reaching toward Cleveland. In the fall, groups leave from the Rattlesnake Springs area on the long march to what is now Oklahoma.

  12. 1850s–1860s

    The railroad and the Civil War

    Railroads meet in Cleveland and the town grows. During the Civil War, Bradley County, like much of East Tennessee, is bitterly divided between Union and Confederacy.

  13. Late 1800s–1900s

    Stoves, mills and churches

    Cleveland becomes a factory town known for its stove makers. The Church of God makes its headquarters here, and Lee University grows up around it.

  14. 1933–1979

    The TVA era

    The Tennessee Valley Authority dams the region’s rivers for power and flood control. Its Tellico Dam, finished in 1979 over Cherokee objections, floods the sites of Chota and Tanasi.

  15. 1984

    Together again at Red Clay

    The Cherokee Nation and the Eastern Band hold a joint council at Red Clay, their first since removal, and light the eternal flame.

  16. 1996

    The Olympics on the Ocoee

    The Ocoee River in neighboring Polk County hosts the Olympic whitewater events.

  17. Today

    Still Cherokee land

    Red Clay hosts Cherokee Days of Recognition and other gatherings, and Cherokee people return to the places their families were taken from. Bradley County is home to more than 100,000 people.

Sources: Bradley County, Cleveland, Tennessee, Red Clay State Historic Park, Rattlesnake Springs, Fort Cass and the Tennessee Encyclopedia. The Cleveland Bradley County Public Library keeps a local history collection. See also today’s entry in On this date, and local history at CBFP.news.

Places to visit and learn

Within a short drive of Cleveland. Hours change by season, so call before you go.

Southern Bradley County · 15 min

Red Clay State Historic Park

The Cherokee Nation’s council grounds from 1832 to 1838, with the Blue Hole Spring, the eternal flame, a council house and farmstead, and a museum. Hosts Cherokee Days of Recognition.

1140 Red Clay Park Rd SW, Cleveland, TN 37311
(423) 478-0339 · tnstateparks.com

Charleston · 20 min

Hiwassee River Heritage Center

On the site of the Cherokee Agency and Fort Cass, the headquarters of the removal. A welcome center and a gateway to the Trail of Tears sites nearby.

8746 Hiwassee St, Charleston, TN 37310
About the center (NPS)

Charleston area

Rattlesnake Springs

Where thousands of Cherokee were held in camps in 1838 and where many detachments set out on the Trail of Tears. On the National Register of Historic Places.

History of the site

Vonore · 1 hr

Sequoyah Birthplace Museum

Owned and run by the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, telling the story of Sequoyah and the syllabary, near the old Overhill towns.

576 Hwy 360, Vonore, TN 37885
(423) 884-6246 · sequoyahmuseum.org

Calhoun, GA · 50 min

New Echota State Historic Site

The Cherokee Nation’s capital before Red Clay, where the Cherokee Phoenix was printed and the 1835 treaty was signed.

gastateparks.org

Cherokee, NC · 2.5 hrs

Museum of the Cherokee People

On the Qualla Boundary, home of the Eastern Band. Thousands of years of Cherokee history, told by the Cherokee.

589 Tsali Blvd, Cherokee, NC 28719
(828) 497-3481 · motcp.org
